# Typography for dyslexic readers

Research ticket: [#11](https://github.com/mhsnook/rsvp-reader/issues/11), part of the Test Mode map [#9](https://github.com/mhsnook/rsvp-reader/issues/9).

## Question

What does primary evidence say about fonts, letter spacing, and text size for dyslexic readers? Does OpenDyslexic outperform ordinary sans and mono fonts? Which findings depend on text size, so that the observed text size context variable gets recorded correctly?

The app today offers three font levels: Space Mono (monospace), Georgia/Times (serif), and OpenDyslexic. Text size is not a test variable. The app derives it from chunk size and viewport width (`src/App.tsx`: single-word mode uses `clamp(1.4rem, 5vw, …)`, chunk mode uses `clamp(1rem, 2.5vw, 1.75rem)` at chunk size 8 or more, `clamp(1.25rem, 3vw, 2.25rem)` at 4 to 7, and `clamp(1.5rem, 3.5vw, 2.75rem)` below 4).

## Summary

1. No controlled study shows OpenDyslexic or Dyslexie reading faster or more accurately than Arial or Times. A 2026 meta-analysis of 15 studies (N = 688) puts the pooled effect at g = -0.04. Readers with dyslexia also rate OpenDyslexic below every ordinary font tested.
2. Among ordinary fonts, sans serif, roman (non-italic), and monospaced fonts gave shorter eye fixations in the one eye-tracking study that compared twelve fonts, but total reading time did not differ except that italics were slower. Arial had the shortest mean reading time; Courier had the shortest fixations; readers preferred Verdana, Helvetica, and Arial.
3. Letter spacing is the one typographic manipulation with repeated positive results. Extra-large spacing (roughly +18% of body size at 14 pt, or +7% to +14% on screen) improved speed or accuracy for dyslexic children and adults in five independent studies. The Dyslexie font's only measured benefit disappeared once Arial was given the same spacing. One study warns that wider letter spacing without wider word spacing slows reading.
4. Larger text helps on screen. Reading improved up to 18 pt and plateaued from 18 to 26 pt in two studies of dyslexic readers. The British Dyslexia Association (BDA) guide's 12 to 14 pt is a floor, not an optimum. Vision science puts the fluent range at an x-height of roughly 0.2 to 2 degrees of visual angle, and rapid serial visual presentation (RSVP) shows the same shape of curve as page reading.
5. Every spacing result was measured at a fixed text size, and crowding depends on angular spacing, so spacing findings carry over only if the app records rendered text size and device size alongside the font.

## Findings

### 1. Dyslexia fonts do not beat ordinary fonts in controlled tests

Every controlled study that pitted OpenDyslexic or Dyslexie against Arial or Times found no advantage on reading speed or accuracy, and most found lower preference.

- Rello and Baeza-Yates 2013, 48 dyslexic readers, 14 pt: OpenDyslexic reading time was 103% of Arial's, inside the noise. The TACCESS 2016 write-up of the same data says: "The fonts designed specifically for dyslexia, OpenDys and OpenDys It., did not lead to better or worse readability." The dyslexic-font factor had no effect on reading time (p = 0.197). Readers with dyslexia rated OpenDyslexic 2.57 out of 5 against 3.60 for the other sans serifs; 47.6% disliked it and 21.4% liked it. Verdana and Helvetica were significantly preferred over OpenDyslexic (p = 0.002 and p = 0.020). ([2013 PDF](https://www.changedyslexia.org/publications/pdfs/2013-ASSETS-Good%20Fonts%20for%20Dyslexia.pdf), [2016 PDF](https://www.superarladislexia.org/pdf/2016-Luz%20Rello-Fonts-taccess.pdf))
- Wery and Diliberto 2017, 12 dyslexic children: "no improvement in reading rate or accuracy for individual students with dyslexia, as well as the group as a whole" for OpenDyslexic against Arial and Times New Roman on letter naming, word reading, and nonsense words. "None of the participants reported preferring to read material presented in that font." Note the confound: OpenDyslexic was set at 10 pt and the others at 12 pt. ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5629233/))
- Kuster et al. 2018, 170 dyslexic Dutch children then 102 dyslexic and 45 typical: Dyslexie was not read faster or more accurately than Arial or Times New Roman with letter size and line spacing matched. In experiment 1, 44.7% to 61.9% preferred Arial versus 20.2% to 37.6% for Dyslexie depending on order; in experiment 2, 11.6% chose Dyslexie. "Preference was not related to reading performance." Conclusion: "the Dyslexie font neither benefits nor impedes the reading process of children with and without dyslexia." ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5934461/))
- Marinus et al. 2016, 39 low-progress readers: Dyslexie was read 7% faster than Arial matched on x-height (75.7 vs 70.4 words per minute, d = 0.70). When Arial's within-word and between-word spacing was matched to Dyslexie's, "the difference in reading speed was no longer significant." The authors conclude the benefit "is not because of its specially designed letter shapes, but because of its particular spacing settings." ([PDF](https://multilit-ecomm-media.s3.ap-southeast-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/15122145/Marinus-et-al-2016-A-special-font-for-people-with-dyslexia.pdf))
- Galliussi et al. 2020, 64 dyslexic and 64 typical children: with spacing held constant, the dyslexia-friendly letterform had no effect on speed (F(1,434) = 0.006, p = 0.936 in the dyslexic group) or accuracy. ([manuscript](https://dsv.units.it/sites/dsv.units.it/files/ric_grpr/ManuscriptANDY2020.pdf))
- Machado et al. 2025, 5 dyslexic adults, nonsense words: "No font type demonstrated superior accuracy or efficiency" across Dyslexie, Times, Arial, and spaced variants. Participants rated spaced Arial highest for satisfaction (4.5) and Dyslexie second (4.2). ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12779878/))
- Azzarello et al. 2026 meta-analysis, 15 studies: "dyslexia-friendly fonts have no consistent or reliable effect on reading performance in terms of speed or accuracy. The overall effect size was negligible, g = -0.04, 95% CI [-0.15, 0.07]." ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42536336/))

OpenDyslexic's own site makes no performance claim; it calls itself "a typeface designed against some common symptoms of dyslexia" and says "If you like the way you are able to read this page, and others, then this typeface is for you!" It is SIL-OFL licensed. ([opendyslexic.org](https://opendyslexic.org/))

### 2. Sans versus serif, mono versus proportional, roman versus italic

The only eye-tracking comparison across many fonts is Rello and Baeza-Yates. All fonts were set at 14 pt, left-aligned, 70 characters per column or fewer, black on white.

Reading time relative to Arial (48 dyslexic readers): Arial 100%, OpenDyslexic 103%, CMU 104%, Courier 104%, OpenDyslexic Italic 105%, Helvetica 109%, Verdana 110%, Times 112%, Times Italic 114%, Myriad 115%, Garamond 117%, Arial Italic 123%. Font type had a significant effect on reading time overall (p < 0.001), but the pairwise wins were mostly against Arial Italic.

Fixation duration (lower is better): Courier 0.22 s, Verdana 0.22, Helvetica 0.24, Arial 0.24, Times 0.25, Myriad 0.25, OpenDyslexic 0.26, Garamond 0.27, CMU 0.27, Arial Italic 0.28.

Style factors:
- Serif: no effect on reading time (p = 0.20); serif fonts had longer fixations (0.26 vs 0.24 s, p = 0.008).
- Monospace: no effect on reading time (p = 0.16); Courier had shorter fixations than the proportional fonts (0.22 vs 0.26 s, p < 0.001). In the 2016 write-up this held for both groups, but readers without dyslexia preferred proportional fonts (3.59 vs 2.85, p = 0.003); readers with dyslexia had no monospace preference (p = 0.79).
- Italic: no effect on reading time (p = 0.09); longer fixations (0.27 vs 0.25 s, p = 0.04). Arial Italic was the worst font on every measure.

Preference, 5-point scale: Verdana 3.79, Helvetica 3.62, Arial 3.60, Times 3.45, Myriad 3.40, CMU 3.31, Courier 3.14, Arial Italic 2.90, Times Italic 2.86, Garamond 2.57, OpenDyslexic 2.57, OpenDyslexic Italic 2.43.

The authors' own caution, from the 2016 discussion: "Although for the tested fonts sans serif, monospaced, and roman fonts led to significantly shorter fixation durations for people with dyslexia, we did not find a significant difference in reading time. Hence, our conclusions on these characteristics are weaker." Their recommendation is "Arial, Courier, CMU, Helvetica, and Verdana" and "roman and sans serif" ([UAIS 2017 Table 3](https://superarladislexia.org/pdf/2017-Luz%20Rello-UAIS_How%20to%20present%20text.pdf)). They also note the serif question has a long history of null results in typical readers.

The BDA guide says "Use sans serif fonts, such as Arial and Comic Sans, as letters can appear less crowded. Alternatives include Verdana, Tahoma, Century Gothic, Trebuchet, Calibri, Open Sans" and "Avoid Underlining and italics as this can make the text appear to run together and cause crowding." The guide cites no studies for the sans serif claim. ([BDA 2018 text](https://www.targetdyslexia.org/app/download/11639293/BDA+Dyslexia+Friendly+Content+-+Fonts.pdf))

Nothing tests Space Mono, Georgia, or any system sans directly. Courier stands in for monospace and Times for serif.

### 3. Letter spacing is the manipulation that repeatedly works

- Zorzi et al. 2012: Times 14 pt with letter spacing widened by 2.5 pt (word and line spacing widened in proportion). Dyslexic children read the spaced text with about half the errors (6 vs 11.8 in experiment 2) and about 0.3 syllables per second faster, which the authors equate to a school year of progress. "The effect of spacing (normal vs. spaced) was significant for dyslexics but not for controls." The gain was largest for children with the poorest letter identification (r = -0.60). The mechanism they propose is crowding. ([PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3396504/))
- Marinus et al. 2016: the Dyslexie advantage above is a spacing advantage. Matching Arial's spacing to Dyslexie removed it. Their spacing conditions added 1.0 to 1.5 pt at an 18 pt Palatino base. ([PDF](https://multilit-ecomm-media.s3.ap-southeast-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/15122145/Marinus-et-al-2016-A-special-font-for-people-with-dyslexia.pdf))
- Sjoblom et al. 2016, adults: "Large letter spacing improves the reading speed in general, as well as improves the reading accuracy in dyslexic readers." ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27629067/))
- Stagg and Kiss 2021, adolescents: "Extra-large letter spacing significantly improved reading speed more substantially for the dyslexia group" and cut missed-word errors. ([PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/34600780/))
- Rello and Baeza-Yates 2017, screen: character spacing of +7% and +14% gave shorter fixations than 0% and -7% when both groups were pooled; within the dyslexic group alone the effect was not significant. Recommendation: "ranging from 0%, +7% to +14%". ([PDF](https://superarladislexia.org/pdf/2017-Luz%20Rello-UAIS_How%20to%20present%20text.pdf))
- Galliussi et al. 2020 is the caution. At 14 pt, adding +0.07 em letter spacing without adding word spacing slowed dyslexic children (3.04 vs 3.22 syllables per second, d = -0.53). Adding both, or word spacing alone, restored speed. "Reading speed is impaired by an increase in inter-letter spacing not combined with an adequate increase in inter-word spacing." Accuracy showed no spacing effects. Their spacing increase was smaller than Zorzi's (+0.98 pt vs +2.5 pt at 14 pt). ([manuscript](https://dsv.units.it/sites/dsv.units.it/files/ric_grpr/ManuscriptANDY2020.pdf))

The BDA guide turns this into rules: "Larger inter-letter / character spacing (sometimes called tracking) improves readability, ideally around 35% of the average letter width. If letter spacing is excessive it can reduce readability. Inter-word spacing should be at least 3.5 times the inter-letter spacing." ([BDA 2018 text](https://www.targetdyslexia.org/app/download/11639293/BDA+Dyslexia+Friendly+Content+-+Fonts.pdf)) The 35% figure has no citation in the guide; it is larger than any tested value above.

What this means for an RSVP reader: in single-word mode there is no word spacing, so the Galliussi hazard does not apply, and Zorzi's crowding argument applies directly to the one word at fixation. In chunk mode the app shows several words, so any letter-spacing level must widen word spacing at least in proportion.

### 4. Text size

- Rello et al. 2013 (Size Matters), 28 dyslexic readers, Wikipedia layout: fixation duration fell with each step from 10 pt to 18 pt and then flattened ("until 18pt font size, the fixation duration decreases with increasing font size"). Comprehension was lowest at 10 pt. Subjective readability rose 10 < 12 < 14 < 18 pt, was flat from 18 to 22 pt, and dropped at 26 pt. Recommendation: 18 pt for web text. Line spacing from 0.8 to 1.8 had no effect. ([PDF](https://pielot.org/pubs/Rello2013-W4A-SizeMatters.pdf))
- Rello and Baeza-Yates 2017: font sizes 18, 22, and 26 pt beat 14 pt on fixation duration for the pooled groups; within the dyslexic group 22 and 26 pt did. Recommendation: "ranging from 18 to 26 points". ([PDF](https://superarladislexia.org/pdf/2017-Luz%20Rello-UAIS_How%20to%20present%20text.pdf))
- BDA: "Font size should be 12-14 point or equivalent (e.g. 1-1.2em / 16-19 px). Some dyslexic readers may request a larger font." ([BDA 2018 text](https://www.targetdyslexia.org/app/download/11639293/BDA+Dyslexia+Friendly+Content+-+Fonts.pdf))
- Legge and Bigelow 2011, typical vision: reading speed is flat across a "fluent range" of x-heights from about 0.2 to 2 degrees of visual angle, and falls sharply below a critical print size of about 0.2 degrees x-height, which is a 9 pt Times body at 40 cm. "Despite the difference in peak reading speeds for RSVP and scrolling text, the range of print sizes for best performance is approximately the same." ([article](https://jov.arvojournals.org/article.aspx?articleid=2191906))

Put together: for typical readers anything above roughly 9 to 12 pt at arm's length is on the plateau, but dyslexic readers kept gaining up to 18 pt on screen. The two dyslexia studies measured fixation duration, not reading time, and the largest sizes lost on preference.

### 5. Which findings depend on text size

The app records text size as an observed context variable downstream of chunk size and device size. These findings are the ones that variable must protect:

1. All spacing effects were measured at one fixed size per study: 14 pt (Zorzi, Galliussi), 18 pt (Marinus), 14 to 26 pt (Rello 2017). Zorzi's +2.5 pt is about 18% of body size; Galliussi's +0.07 em is 7%. The crowding mechanism is about angular gap between letters, so the same CSS `letter-spacing` in em gives a different angular gap at each rendered pixel size and viewing distance. A spacing level only compares across sessions if rendered pixel size and device class are logged with it.
2. The Galliussi hazard (letter spacing without word spacing slows reading) appeared at 14 pt with a small increment. Whether it holds at the app's larger single-word sizes is untested.
3. Dyslexia fonts render at a different apparent size from ordinary fonts at the same nominal size. Marinus: "12-point Arial differs from 12-point Dyslexie not only in letter shapes but also in display letter size and spacing." Wery set OpenDyslexic at 10 pt against 12 pt for the others; Kuster set Dyslexie at 12 or 13 pt against Arial at 13 or 16 pt to match x-height. The app sets all three fonts at the same CSS size, so the OpenDyslexic level differs from the other levels in rendered x-height as well as letterform. Record the font with the CSS size; measuring OpenDyslexic's x-height ratio against Space Mono and Georgia is a small follow-up (fontTools on `public/fonts/OpenDyslexic-Regular.otf`).
4. Font size itself interacts with chunk size in the app. At chunk size 8 or more the text drops to `clamp(1rem, 2.5vw, 1.75rem)`; on a 375 px phone that is 16 px, which is 12 pt, near the BDA floor and below the 18 pt where Rello's dyslexic readers stopped improving. At 40 cm a 12 pt sans with a 0.5 x-height ratio is about 0.24 degrees, just above Legge's critical print size; at a closer phone distance it is larger. So a chunk-size effect may partly be a text-size effect. The trial should log computed font size in px per session (or per chunk-size setting) plus viewport width and device pixel ratio, not just the chunk size.
5. The font-type results (section 2) were all at 14 pt on a 17-inch 1024x768 monitor. Nobody has shown that Courier's fixation advantage or Arial's reading-time advantage survives at 40 px single-word RSVP sizes.

Minimum record per reading session: font level, chunk size, computed font size in CSS px for the display element, viewport width and height, `devicePixelRatio`, device class. Letter spacing in em if a spaced level is added.

Ordered from best-supported to least. Each level names the evidence for keeping it in the trial.

Machado 2025 adults rated spaced Arial highest. | Not in the app. Galliussi 2020: letter spacing alone can slow reading in multi-word text. | **Serif (current Georgia/Times level)** | Times read at 112% of Arial and CMU at 104% in Rello 2013 with no significant serif effect on reading time; Times was fourth in preference. | **OpenDyslexic (current level)** | Free, SIL-OFL, already shipped. Some readers ask for it, and Test Mode measures rather than assumes. Levels not worth adding: italic of anything (worst on every measure), Comic Sans (BDA recommends it but no study tested it), Dyslexie (commercial licence and the same null results as OpenDyslexic).

### 7. Evidence strength

The evidence against dyslexia fonts is strong for a typography question: six independent controlled studies in four languages and a 2026 meta-analysis all land on no effect, the confounds run in the font's favour where they exist, and the one apparent benefit (Marinus) was traced to spacing. The evidence for letter spacing is moderate: five studies with positive results, one PNAS study with a large effect in children, but effect sizes shrink in adults, the increments and base sizes vary, and Galliussi shows the effect can reverse when word spacing is not widened with it. The evidence for text size is moderate for "bigger than 14 pt helps dyslexic readers on screen" but rests on two eye-tracking studies from one lab measuring fixation duration, not reading speed. The evidence on sans versus serif and mono versus proportional is weak: one lab, one experiment, 14 pt, fixation duration differences without reading-time differences, and the authors say so. Nothing in this literature tests RSVP presentation, single-word display, or text sizes above 26 pt, so every carry-over to this app is an extrapolation. The BDA guide is a practice document; it cites no studies for its font or spacing figures, and its 35% tracking figure exceeds any tested value.
